What is BOQ vs actual cost?

BOQ vs actual cost compares each item's planned quantity and rate in the Bill of Quantities with the quantity actually executed and the rate actually paid. The difference splits into a quantity variance (the site used more or less) and a rate variance (you paid more or less). Tracked during the job, it shows an overrun while there is still budget left to save.

Most "BOQ vs actual" reports show one total and stop there. That total can't tell you whether the problem is on site or in purchasing, and the fix is different for each.

BOQ vs budget vs committed vs actual

Four numbers get mixed up in cost meetings. Keep them separate:

NumberWhat it isWhere it comes from
BOQ valueContract quantity × contract rateThe BOQ in the work order
Budget (cost)What you planned to spend to execute itYour estimate or rate analysis
Committed costMoney you've promised but not yet spentPurchase orders and subcontract work orders
Actual costMoney spent on work done so farGRNs and material issues, attendance, machine hours, site expenses

BOQ vs actual compares the first and last. Committed cost is your early warning: a PO raised today at a higher rate is next month's overrun.

BOQ vs actual cost formulas

On this page, a positive variance means an overrun (actual is above BOQ). Earned-value reports use the opposite sign, so check which one your team uses before comparing reports.

Total variance   = Actual cost − BOQ cost
Quantity variance = (Actual qty − BOQ qty) × BOQ rate
Rate variance    = (Actual rate − BOQ rate) × Actual qty
Quantity variance + Rate variance = Total variance
Variance %       = Total variance ÷ BOQ cost × 100

What the split tells you: the site actually saved half a tonne of steel, but a ₹3,000/t price rise wiped it out. Of the ₹1,06,400 overrun, ₹90,900 (85%) is rate, not usage. That's a purchasing conversation, not a site one. A single "+5.6%" total hides all of this.

Comparing BOQ and actual cost before the job is finished

The most common mistake: comparing actual cost to date against the whole BOQ. At 40% complete, of course you've spent less than the full BOQ. That says nothing. Compare actual cost against the BOQ value of the work actually done (the earned value).

BOQ value of the whole job (BAC) = ₹5.00 Cr
Work done (measured)            = 40% → earned value ₹2.00 Cr
Actual cost to date             = ₹2.20 Cr
Overrun so far                  = ₹2.20 − ₹2.00 = ₹20 lakh
Cost performance index (CPI) = 2.00 ÷ 2.20 = 0.91
Estimate at completion (EAC)  = 5.00 ÷ 0.91 ≈ ₹5.50 Cr
Cost to complete                = 5.50 − 2.20 ≈ ₹3.30 Cr

If nothing changes, a ₹20 lakh overrun at 40% becomes about ₹50 lakh at handover. That's why the comparison needs to happen monthly, not at final bill.

Where the actual numbers come from

BOQ vs actual is only as good as the actuals. On most Indian sites they come from this chain:

BOQ and work order

Items, units, contract quantities and rates. See work order vs BOQ.

Indent and purchase order (committed)

The rate you've agreed to pay before material arrives.

GRN and site issue (actual material)

What arrived, at what rate, and what was issued to which work.

Attendance, machine hours, site expenses (actual labour and plant)

Who worked, which equipment ran, and what petty cash was spent.

Measurement book and RA bill (actual quantity)

What was executed and billed. See the measurement book and RA bill software.

If any link in the chain lives in a WhatsApp group or a notebook, the actuals arrive late or not at all. That is usually why BOQ vs actual is done at month-end in Excel, when it's too late to act.

Why it matters

Cost overruns are the norm on large Indian projects, not the exception. MoSPI's July 2026 project monitoring data, reported by Business Standard, put the cumulative overrun across 1,775 central infrastructure projects worth ₹150 crore or more at about ₹3.41 lakh crore, with original cost ₹33,70,138 crore against revised cost ₹37,10,642 crore. The monitored set changes every month, so treat this as a snapshot, not a trend. The lesson for a contractor of any size is the same: you can only fix an overrun you can see.

Frequently asked questions

What is BOQ vs actual cost?

It's the comparison between the planned quantity and rate in your Bill of Quantities and the quantity actually executed and the rate actually paid. The gap is your cost variance, split into a quantity variance and a rate variance.

How do you calculate cost variance in construction?

Total variance = actual cost − BOQ cost. Split it into quantity variance = (actual qty − BOQ qty) × BOQ rate, and rate variance = (actual rate − BOQ rate) × actual qty. The two add up to the total variance.

What is the difference between quantity variance and rate variance?

Quantity variance shows whether the site used more or less than the BOQ, which usually points to wastage, rework or measurement. Rate variance shows whether you paid more or less per unit, which usually points to purchasing or market prices.

How do I compare BOQ and actual cost when the project is only partly done?

Compare actual cost to date with the BOQ value of the work actually done (earned value), not with the whole BOQ. CPI = earned value ÷ actual cost, and estimate at completion = BOQ value ÷ CPI.

What is the difference between committed cost and actual cost?

Committed cost is money you've promised through purchase orders and subcontract work orders but not yet spent. Actual cost is what has been spent on work done. Committed cost shows next month's overrun before it happens.

Can I track BOQ vs actual cost in Excel?

Yes, for a small single job, if someone re-types measurements, GRNs and attendance every week. Across several sites the re-typing falls behind, which is why most firms move to software that builds actuals from site entries.

How are the BOQ, measurement book and RA bill linked?

The measurement book records executed quantity against BOQ items. Measured quantities flow into the abstract and the RA bill at the BOQ rate. The same measured quantities are the actual-quantity side of BOQ vs actual.
