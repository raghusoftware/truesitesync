Why diesel disappears — and nobody notices

On a site running excavators, tippers, DG sets and a batching plant, diesel is a huge running cost — and one of the easiest to steal, because nobody's watching the maths. Fuel is bought in bulk, poured into machines by operators, and logged (if at all) in a torn register in a cabin. There's no link between litres bought, litres issued, and hours actually run. So a hundred litres can go missing a week — siphoned, over-recorded, or sold — and it hides inside a fuel bill that always looks "about right".

The theft isn't usually dramatic. It's a steady 5–15% skim that's invisible without reconciliation. And you can't stop what you can't measure. The good news: fuel theft dies the moment every litre is tied to a machine and every machine's consumption is checked against the hours it worked. That's a data problem, and it has a clean solution.

The five controls that stop fuel theft

Log every litre issued

Record diesel issued to a specific machine, every time — no fuel leaves the store untracked.

Capture running hours

Log opening/closing meter readings per machine, so consumption has a denominator.

Watch litres-per-hour

The software computes L/hr per machine; anything above the norm is flagged for a look.

Reconcile purchases

Match diesel purchased against diesel issued, so bulk-buy skimming has nowhere to hide.

Make it routine, not heroic

Because logging takes seconds on a phone, the controls actually get used every day.

Stopping fuel theft — FAQs

How do you stop fuel theft on a construction site?

By tying every litre of diesel to a machine and checking each machine's consumption against the hours it actually ran. When diesel issued is logged per machine and litres-per-hour is computed, abnormal consumption is flagged and theft becomes visible — which both catches and deters it.

What is litres-per-hour and why does it matter?

Litres-per-hour is diesel consumed divided by running hours for a machine. Each machine type has a normal range, so a figure well above it points to theft, a leak, or a fault. It's the single most useful number for exposing fuel loss.

How much fuel is typically lost to theft?

On unmonitored sites, a steady 5–15% skim is common — small enough to hide inside a fuel bill that looks about right, but large enough to matter across a fleet over a year.

Do I need special hardware or fuel sensors?

No. You can stop most fuel theft with disciplined logging alone — diesel issued per machine and running hours — captured on a phone. Sensors can add precision later, but the reconciliation approach works without them.

Does simply tracking fuel actually reduce theft?

Yes. A large part of the reduction comes from the deterrent effect: once operators know every litre is logged and outliers are questioned, the easy skim stops. Many sites see consumption drop in the first month of reconciliation.
